Welcome

The fundamentals, used properly, are the advanced capabilities

Most people use Copilot as a smarter search box. This session is about the four things that separate a throwaway answer from work you would actually put your name on — and you’ll run them on your own laptop as we go.

How this page runsTwo kinds of moment. Watch = we drive it on screen. Try it = you run it on your machine. Every prompt has a Copy button — take them with you, they still work tomorrow.
About the data you’ll useUse your own work wherever you can. These prompts are far more convincing on your real week than on a sample. Where a file is genuinely needed, we use a fictional carrier — Lone Star Air — so nothing sensitive goes on screen. It’s in the resource panel, ready to download.
One ground ruleCopilot drafts. You decide. It works inside Southwest’s existing permissions — it only ever sees what you already have access to — but it can still be confidently wrong. Open a source before you forward anything.
The pattern

Goal · Context · Source · Expectations

One pattern underneath every prompt on this page. Tell Copilot what you want, the situation you’re in, what to ground on, and what a good answer looks like. Four parts. That’s the whole technique.

GOAL
what you want done CONTEXT
the situation and who it’s for SOURCE
the files, mail, meetings to use EXPECTATIONS
what a good output looks like

Read the pattern in action

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Prepare me a short written brief on the initiative I own that is most likely to come up this week. I have a leadership check-in on Thursday, the audience is cross-functional, and they have fifteen minutes and one decision to make. Ground it in my email threads, chats and meetings from the past two weeks where I am a participant, and cite the source behind each point. Open with the decision you need from them, then three key points, two risks, and a recommended next step. Keep it under 200 words, plain language, and flag anything you are inferring rather than confirming.

The colours above are the same four parts, highlighted inside the prompt. Every prompt in this walkthrough carries the strip, so you can see the pattern rather than memorise it. Expectations is the part almost everyone skips — people tell Copilot what to do, but never what “good” looks like, then blame the answer.

  • State the Goal.One clear verb. “Prepare a brief” beats “help me with the leadership thing.”
  • Give the Context.Who it’s for, when it’s needed, what’s at stake. This is what turns a generic answer into yours.
  • Name the Source.The meeting, the thread, the file, the site. And ask it to cite — so you can check it.
  • Set the Expectations.Structure, length, tone, what to include and what to leave out. Be blunt about format.
Try this right nowTake a prompt you already use and add the two parts you skipped. Most people already write a Goal and a Source. Add Context and Expectations, re-run the same request, and the difference is usually obvious in one go.
Capability 1 · you try this

Personalization & memory

Tell Copilot who you are once, and it holds on to it across every future chat. This is the highest-return five minutes in the whole session — and almost nobody does it.

Try it yourself~5 minCopilot Chat
Two ways in — pick one, not both.

Either answer five quick questions, or let Copilot read your actual work and tell you what it already knows about you. Both end in the same place: instructions that persist across every chat. Start a new chat first.

CHOOSE ONE STARTING POINT

Option A  Have it interview you

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Help me personalize Copilot for my role so you respond the same way in every future chat without me re-explaining who I am. I work at Southwest Airlines and I want this set up properly once, today. Ask me five questions, one at a time, and wait for my answer before asking the next: my role and team, my top priorities this quarter, the people and groups I work with most, the kinds of documents I produce, and the tone I prefer. Then draft my personalization instructions, under 200 words, written in the first person in my voice, ready to paste straight into Copilot’s settings.

Best when you want full control over what Copilot knows about you, or your last month of work isn’t representative of the job you actually do.

OR

Option B  Let it read your work first

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Build my Copilot personalization from what you can already see about my work — I do not want a long interview. I work at Southwest Airlines and I would rather correct your draft than answer twenty questions. Use my work profile and my email, meetings, chats and files from the last 30 days. First give me up to ten short statements about my role, priorities, main collaborators and the documents I produce, and label each one [Profile], [Recent work] or [Assumption]. Then ask me only the two questions you still genuinely need. Then draft my personalization instructions, under 200 words, in the first person in my voice, ready to paste into settings.

This is the one worth showing people. It reads your real working context, separates what it knows from what it is guessing, and only asks what it genuinely cannot work out.

THEN, WHICHEVER YOU CHOSE

Try it  Sharpen it before you save

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Tighten those personalization instructions so they actually change how you answer me. Right now they read like something that could apply to any employee, and that means they will change nothing. Work only from the draft you just produced. Keep my role, priorities and key collaborators. Add how I want you to handle uncertainty, that I want sources named whenever you use my files or meetings, and my default format — short, structured, decision first. Cut anything generic. Return the final version only, ready to paste.

Try it  Prove it actually took

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Tell me in one line what you now understand about me, then draft a three-sentence update on what I should focus on this week. You are now running with my saved personalization, and I want to hear the difference. Base the update on my recent meetings and email. Point out which parts of your answer came from my personalization rather than from the source material, and answer exactly the way my settings tell you to.

If you cannot hear the difference, it is not specific enough yet. Go back and add the thing that makes your job different from the person sitting next to you.

  • Start a new chat.So the setup isn’t coloured by whatever you were doing earlier.
  • Pick Option A or B.Two routes to the same place. B is the one that surprises people.
  • Sharpen it.Delete anything that would be true of any other Southwest employee.
  • Save it.Paste the final version into Copilot’s personalization / custom instructions.
  • Prove it works.Run the last prompt. You should be able to point at the change.
Don’t stop at “drafted”Leave the room with it saved and running. A draft sitting in a chat window does nothing. Ninety seconds of pasting is the entire payoff.
Capability 2 · you try this

Grounding — point it at the right thing

The single biggest difference between a vague answer and a useful one is what you told it to read. Copilot can reach your mail, meetings, chats, files and the people around you — but only if you say which.

Try it yourself~7 minCopilot Chat · Outlook · Teams
Say where to look

“Use the thread with…”, “from Tuesday’s meeting…”, “in this file…” Vague in, vague out.

Say how far back

“Last 30 days” beats no window at all. It stops Copilot dragging in stale context.

Ask it to cite

If it names the source, you can check it in ten seconds. If it can’t, that tells you something too.

Try it  1 · Catch up on what you missed

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Identify the five developments most likely to need my attention, and tell me what each one needs from me. I have been heads-down and I am catching up after a busy week. Review my work from the past five business days across email, Teams chats, meetings and files. For each one: what changed, why it matters now, the decision or next step it may need, and the source that supports it. Put the most time-sensitive first, keep each to two or three lines, and separate confirmed facts from things you are inferring.

Try it  2 · Pull a meeting apart properly

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Turn my most recent recorded meeting into something I can act on. I need to follow through today and I do not want to re-watch it. Use the transcript and any files shared in that meeting. Give me: decisions made and who made them, open questions nobody answered, actions with an owner and a date, and anything said that contradicts something said earlier in the meeting. Mark anything where the transcript is ambiguous rather than guessing.

“Anything that contradicts something said earlier” is the line worth stealing. It is the part a human recap almost always misses.

Try it  3 · Get up to speed on a thread you joined late

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Bring me up to speed on this email thread and tell me what is actually being asked of me. I was added part-way through and I do not have the history the others have. Use the full thread, including the earlier messages before I was added, plus anything attached. Give me a five-line history, the current position of each participant, the open question, and a suggested reply I can edit. Flag anyone whose position you had to infer.
Watch-outCopilot only sees what you can already see. If an answer looks thin, it is usually because the source you named is not shared with you — not because Copilot is being unhelpful. Check where the file actually lives.
Capability 3 · you try this

Working across your applications

Most real work isn’t one prompt — it’s a chain. Gather something, shape it, deliver it. Copilot carries the context from one step to the next, so you never re-explain yourself. Run this end to end.

Try it yourself~8 minChat → Word → PowerPoint
Copilot Chat Word PowerPoint Outlook Teams Excel

Try it  1 · Gather — find the signal

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Pull together the current state of the initiative I own that has moved most in the last two weeks. I need to brief my leader on it and I have lost track of the detail. Use my email, chats, meetings and files from the last 14 days, and cite each point. Give me: where it stands, what changed, what is blocked, who owns what, and the one decision that would unblock it. Keep it to bullets and mark anything you are inferring.

Try it  2 · Shape — draft the written update

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Turn what you just found into a written update for my direct leader. They have fifteen minutes and need to leave with one decision made. Use only the points you just gave me, and keep the sources next to each one. Lead with the decision needed, then progress, then open questions. Under 200 words, neutral and direct, no padding, and no adjectives you cannot evidence.

Open this one in Word. Ask Copilot in Word to draft from the update — you will be editing a document, not copying text out of a chat window.

Try it  3 · Deliver — turn it into slides

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Turn the update you just wrote into three slides. I am presenting these live to that same leadership group, so the wording has to be tight enough to say out loud. Use only what is in the update — do not add new claims or numbers. One headline decision slide, one progress slide, one risks-and-next-steps slide. Maximum four bullets per slide, under twelve words each, plus a one-line speaker note per slide.

Notice what makes this a chain. Step 2 uses step 1’s output as its Source, and step 3 uses step 2’s. You state the context once. That is the whole technique — and it is why the answers get better as you go rather than worse.

Watch-outAn error in step one travels all the way to your slides. Chaining works precisely because each step trusts the last. Open at least one cited source before you present it.

The same shape, different starting points

The chain you just ran starts from your week. Almost all repeated work follows the same three beats — gather → shape → deliver — it just starts somewhere else. Find yours.

Start from your weekThe one you just ran — any recurring status or check-in.
Catch up on 5 days→Leader update→Slides
Start from a documentA report, contract or policy lands and something has to come out of it.
Summarize the file→Pull the implications→Draft the response
Start from a meetingDecisions get made and the follow-through is the actual work.
Recap & decisions→Actions by owner→Follow-up note
Start from dataThe numbers arrive and someone has to explain them.
Analyze the export→Explain what moved→Commentary for review

Try it  Find your own chain

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Find the multi-step outputs I produce again and again — where I gather something, shape it, then deliver it to someone. Give me my top three. I want to hand one of these to Copilot permanently, starting today. Look at my work over the last 30 days across email, meetings, chats and files. For each, show it as a three-step chain, say what triggers it, who receives the final output, and write the first prompt I would run to start it. Then tell me which of the three to hand over first and why. Keep it practical — I want to act on this today, not read an analysis.
Capability 4 · watch, then try

Inside the file — ask it, or let it edit

Everything so far happened in chat. This is the shift most people miss: Copilot lives inside Excel, Word and PowerPoint too — and there are two very different ways to use it. Knowing which one you want is the whole skill.

Ask Copilot — you keep the pen

You ask a question about the file. Copilot answers in the side pane: explains, analyses, summarises, suggests. Nothing in the file changes unless you decide to change it.

You ask→It answers in the pane→You decide
Edit with Copilot — it holds the pen

You describe the change you want made. Copilot works directly on the document or workbook — adding the column, rewriting the section, restructuring the deck — and shows you what it did so you can accept or undo.

You describe the change→It edits the file→You accept or undo
The rule of thumbAsk when you don’t trust it yet. Edit when you do. Analysis, exploration and anything going to a Customer or a regulator — ask, then make the change yourself. Formatting, restructuring, first drafts and repetitive cleanup — let it edit. And work on a copy the first few times.

Sample workbook for this section

LoneStar-Air-Q3-Network-Data.xlsx — a quarter of route, delay, customer and turn-time data for a fictional carrier. Download it from the resource panel and open it in Excel. Nothing in it is Southwest data, so it is safe to project, share and upload.

Ask Copilot in Excel~6 minExcel

Try it  1 · Understand the file before you touch it

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Tell me what is actually in this workbook and what it can and cannot answer. I have just been handed it and I do not want to build an analysis on a column I have misread. Use every sheet in this workbook, including the Read Me. For each sheet: what one row represents, the grain of the data, which columns are measures versus attributes, and anything that looks inconsistent or incomplete. Then list five questions this data can answer well and three it cannot.

Start here, every time. The most common Copilot-in-Excel mistake is not a wrong formula — it is confidently analysing a column that does not mean what you assumed.

Try it  2 · Ask the question you actually care about

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Show me which routes lost contribution margin between July and September, and explain what drove it. I have to explain the quarter to a leadership group that will push back on anything that sounds like a guess. Use the Route Performance sheet, and cross-check anything operational against the Delay Causes and Turn Performance sheets. Give me the five biggest decliners with the dollar movement, then for each say whether the driver looks like fare, load factor, cost or operational reliability — and name the columns you used. Say clearly where the data is not sufficient to attribute a cause.

Try it  3 · Make it explain its own maths

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Walk me through exactly how you calculated the numbers in your last answer. Someone senior is going to ask me how this was worked out and I need to answer without hedging. Use only the calculation you just performed. Give me the formula or logic per figure, the columns and rows it used, any rows you excluded and why, and one sentence on where this method would break down.

This is the trust-building prompt. Ask it of every number you plan to repeat out loud. If the explanation does not hold up, you have found the problem before your audience did.

NOW HAND IT THE PEN
Edit with Copilot~6 minExcel · Word · PowerPoint
Before you run theseMake a copy of the workbook first. These prompts change the file. That is the point — but you want your first few goes to be on something disposable.

Try it  4 · Let it restructure the sheet

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Add a summary sheet to this workbook that a leader could read in ninety seconds. This file is currently four raw data sheets and nobody outside the team can use it. Build it from the Route Performance, Delay Causes and Turn Performance sheets. One sheet named Summary, with: total departures, on-time percentage, load factor, revenue and margin by month; the top five and bottom five routes by margin; and the three stations with the most delay minutes. Add a short written takeaway under each block. Use clear headers and keep the formatting plain.

Try it  5 · Let it do the tedious part

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Add the derived columns I keep rebuilding by hand, and flag the rows worth looking at. I rebuild these same calculations every month and it is the least valuable hour of my week. Work on the Route Performance sheet. Add columns for revenue per departure, margin per departure, and margin as a percentage of revenue. Then add a Flag column that marks any row where on-time percentage is below 75 or margin per departure is negative. Use real formulas rather than pasted values, so the sheet still works when the data refreshes.

“Use real formulas rather than pasted values” is the line that turns a one-off answer into something reusable. Without it you often get static numbers.

Try it  6 · Same idea, different app

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Rewrite this document so it opens with the decision instead of the background. The reader is senior, short of time, and will stop after the first paragraph if it is not clear what I need from them. Work on the document that is open, and keep every fact and figure exactly as written. Move the recommendation to the top, cut the history to three lines, keep the detail but push it below a clear heading, and hold it to one page. Do not introduce any claim that is not already in the document — tell me if something is missing rather than inventing it.

Try this one in Word, then try the equivalent in PowerPoint. The pattern is identical across apps — describe the change and the constraint, then check what it did.

The habit that keeps you safeRead the change before you accept it. Edit with Copilot is fast enough that it is tempting to keep going. Spot-check one number and one sentence every time — it takes seconds, and it is the difference between a tool you trust and a tool that embarrasses you.
Where this goes next

Make it stick

Nothing you did today matters unless it survives contact with next week. Pick a small number of things and make them automatic — that is how this becomes normal rather than a session you attended once.

Today

Save your personalization. That is it — if you only do one thing, do that. Everything else gets better because of it.

This week

Run the catch-up prompt on Monday morning and the meeting prompt after your next recorded meeting. Two prompts, twice.

This month

Take the one chain you identified and run it every single time that work comes round. Repetition is what turns it into a habit.

The four things worth remembering

Goal · Context · Source · Expectations

If an answer disappoints you, one of the four is missing. Nine times out of ten it is Expectations.

Name the source, ask for the citation

The fastest way to know whether to trust an answer — and the fastest way to catch it when it is wrong.

Chain instead of restarting

Build on the last answer rather than opening a new chat. The context you already gave is the expensive part.

Ask when unsure, edit when confident

Both live inside Word, Excel and PowerPoint. Choosing the right one is most of the skill.

Try it  Your personal next-seven-days plan

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Build me a seven-day plan for using you properly, based on the work I actually have coming up. I have just been through a hands-on Copilot session and I want this to stick rather than fade by Friday. Use my calendar for the next seven days, plus my email and files from the last two weeks. Give me one specific thing per day, tied to something real on my calendar, with the exact prompt to run and roughly how long it will take. Nothing longer than ten minutes. Order it so the easiest wins come first.
If you take one thing awayThe gap between people who get value from Copilot and people who don’t isn’t access — it’s specificity. Same licence, same data, same tool. The difference is whether you told it what you actually wanted.
